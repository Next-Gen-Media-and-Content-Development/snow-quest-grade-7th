Introduction to Acids, Bases & Neutral Substances

🧪

All substances around us can be grouped into three types based on their chemical nature — Acidic, Basic (Alkaline), and Neutral. Indicators help us identify which group a substance belongs to.

1
Acidic Substances

These substances taste sour and turn blue litmus red. Examples: lemon juice, vinegar, tamarind, amla, curd.

  • Contain hydrogen ions (H⁺)
  • pH less than 7
  • Turn blue litmus → red
2
Basic Substances

These substances taste bitter, feel soapy and turn red litmus blue. Examples: baking soda, soap, lime water, antacids.

  • Contain hydroxide ions (OH⁻)
  • pH greater than 7
  • Turn red litmus → blue
3
Neutral Substances

These substances do not affect litmus paper. Examples: pure water, salt solution, cooking oil.

  • pH exactly 7
  • Do not change litmus colour
All acidic and basic substances show their character through INDICATORS — special substances that change colour in the presence of acids or bases.

Natural Indicators

🌿

Indicators are substances that change colour when added to an acid or a base. Many natural materials can be used as indicators.

1
Litmus

Litmus is extracted from lichens. It is the most commonly used indicator. It is available as litmus solution and litmus paper (red and blue).

  • Blue litmus → turns RED in acid
  • Red litmus → turns BLUE in base
  • No change in neutral substances
2
Turmeric (Haldi)

Turmeric paper is made by soaking strips of white paper in turmeric solution and drying them. It turns red/brown in basic solutions.

  • Stays yellow in acid and neutral
  • Turns red-brown in base
✏️ Interesting: Soap stains on yellow turmeric turn red because soap is basic!
3
China Rose (Gudhal)

A pink/red extract from China rose petals acts as an indicator. It changes colour depending on the nature of the solution.

  • Turns dark pink/magenta in acid
  • Turns green in base
  • Stays pink in neutral
Natural indicators made at home: red cabbage juice, grape juice, purple carrot juice — all change colour with acids and bases!

Acids Around Us

🍋

Acidic substances are found everywhere in daily life — in the food we eat, in our bodies, and in nature. They share common properties.

1
Common Acids in Food

Many foods we eat contain natural acids. These give them a sour taste and help in preservation.

  • Lemon / Amla — Citric acid
  • Tamarind / Raw mango — Tartaric acid
  • Curd / Buttermilk — Lactic acid
  • Vinegar — Acetic acid
  • Tomato — Oxalic acid
2
Properties of Acids

All acids share certain common properties which help identify them.

  • Taste sour (never taste strong acids!)
  • Turn blue litmus paper red
  • React with metals to produce hydrogen gas
  • pH value less than 7
Hydrochloric acid (HCl) is present in our stomach and helps in digestion. It is a strong acid — never taste or touch lab acids directly!

❓ Quick Check: What acid is present in curd and why does it taste sour?

✅ Curd contains Lactic acid. All acids taste sour because they release hydrogen ions (H⁺) in solution.

Bases Around Us

🧼

Basic (alkaline) substances are also found in many everyday items. They taste bitter, feel slippery or soapy, and turn red litmus blue.

1
Common Bases at Home

Many household substances are basic in nature. They are used for cleaning and cooking.

  • Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate)
  • Washing soda (sodium carbonate)
  • Soap and detergent
  • Lime water (calcium hydroxide)
  • Milk of magnesia (antacid)
2
Properties of Bases

All bases share certain identifying properties.

  • Taste bitter (never taste lab bases!)
  • Feel slippery or soapy to touch
  • Turn red litmus paper blue
  • pH value greater than 7
✏️ Turmeric stains on white cloth turn red when soap (a base) is applied — a natural base test!
Bases that dissolve in water are called ALKALIS. Lime water (Ca(OH)₂) turns milky when CO₂ is passed through it — a standard test for carbon dioxide!

❓ Quick Check: Why does soap feel slippery? What does it tell us about its nature?

✅ Soap feels slippery because it is basic in nature. All bases have a soapy, slippery feel — this is a characteristic property of bases.

Neutralization Reaction

⚗️

When an acid and a base react with each other, they cancel out each other's properties. This reaction is called neutralization, and it produces a salt and water.

1
The Reaction

Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat

  • The acid's H⁺ ions combine with base's OH⁻ ions
  • They form water (H₂O)
  • The remaining ions form salt
  • Heat is released (exothermic reaction)
2
Real-life Applications

Neutralization reactions are used to solve many practical problems.

  • Antacid tablets neutralize excess stomach acid
  • Lime (base) added to acidic soil makes it fertile
  • Baking soda (base) used when an ant bites (ant injects formic acid)
  • Wasp sting is basic — treated with vinegar (acid)
Acid + Base → Salt + Water  |  HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O
Ant bite injects FORMIC ACID → apply baking soda (base) to neutralize. Wasp sting is BASIC → apply vinegar (acid). Know your stings!

Salts and Their Importance

🧂

Salts are formed when an acid reacts with a base. The most familiar salt is common salt (sodium chloride, NaCl) — essential for life.

1
Common Salt (NaCl)

Sodium chloride is formed when hydrochloric acid reacts with sodium hydroxide. It is used in food, preservation, and industry.

  • Used as food seasoning and preservative
  • Used in making soap, glass, and paper
  • Needed by the human body for nerve function
  • Source: sea water, rock salt mines
2
Other Important Salts

Many salts have specific uses in daily life and industry.

  • Baking soda (NaHCO₃) — used in cooking and antacids
  • Washing soda (Na₂CO₃) — used for cleaning
  • Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O) — for casts in hospitals
  • Potassium nitrate (KNO₃) — used in fertilizers
India is one of the largest producers of salt in the world. Most of our salt comes from sea water through solar evaporation in places like Gujarat and Tamil Nadu.

The pH Scale

📏

pH is a scale from 0 to 14 that measures how acidic or basic a solution is. The word "pH" stands for "potential of Hydrogen."

1
Understanding pH Values
  • pH 0–6 → Acidic (lower = stronger acid)
  • pH 7 → Neutral (pure water)
  • pH 8–14 → Basic/Alkaline (higher = stronger base)
  • Human blood: pH 7.35–7.45 (slightly basic)
  • Stomach acid: pH 1.5–3.5 (strongly acidic)
2
pH of Common Substances
  • Battery acid: pH ~1 · Lemon juice: pH ~2
  • Vinegar: pH ~3 · Tomato: pH ~4
  • Coffee: pH ~5 · Milk: pH ~6.5
  • Pure water: pH 7 · Seawater: pH ~8
  • Baking soda: pH ~9 · Milk of magnesia: pH ~10
  • Bleach: pH ~12 · Drain cleaner: pH ~14
Rain water with pH below 5.6 is called ACID RAIN. It forms when pollutants like SO₂ and NO₂ from factories mix with water vapour in clouds.

Applications & Chapter Summary

✨

Acids, bases and salts have countless real-life applications — from agriculture and medicine to food and industry. Let us review the complete chapter.

1
Applications in Daily Life
  • Soil pH correction: acidic soil → add lime (Ca(OH)₂); basic soil → add gypsum
  • Tooth decay: bacteria produce acid → fluoride toothpaste (basic) helps
  • Indigestion: excess HCl in stomach → antacid (Mg(OH)₂) neutralizes it
  • Insect stings: ant (formic acid) → use base; bee (formic acid) → use base
  • Factory waste: acidic effluents neutralized before releasing into water bodies
2
Chapter Summary — Quick Revision
  • Substances: Acidic (pH<7), Neutral (pH=7), Basic (pH>7)
  • Indicators: Litmus, Turmeric, China Rose detect acid/base
  • Acids: sour taste, blue→red litmus, H⁺ ions
  • Bases: bitter, soapy, red→blue litmus, OH⁻ ions
  • Neutralization: Acid + Base → Salt + Water
  • pH scale: 0 (most acidic) to 14 (most basic)
Acharya P. C. Ray (1861–1944), the "Father of Modern Indian Chemistry," founded India's first pharmaceutical company. His research on mercury compounds is legendary!

❓ Quick Check: Why is lime (calcium hydroxide) added to acidic soil? What reaction takes place?

✅ Lime is a base. When added to acidic soil, it neutralizes the excess acid (neutralization reaction: Acid + Base → Salt + Water), raising the pH to a level where crops can grow well.
