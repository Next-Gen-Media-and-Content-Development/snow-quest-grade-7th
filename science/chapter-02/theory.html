Science Fair Mystery: Hidden Ink and Acids/Bases

A plain white sheet can suddenly shout “Welcome!” when science tricks are involved!

At the science fair, Ashwin and Keerthi received a white paper that looked empty, but after a volunteer sprayed a liquid, a message appeared. This happens when the paper has “hidden ink” that reacts with the sprayed liquid (often an indicator or an acidic/basic solution) to form a coloured change.

This chapter helps us explore three kinds of substances—acidic, basic, and neutral—by using indicators. Indicators are special materials that change colour when they meet an acid or a base, helping us identify what kind of substance we are testing.

By doing simple tests and grouping results carefully, we can classify everyday liquids and solutions without tasting them (never taste unknown chemicals!).

Indicators show acids and bases by colour changeAcids, bases, and neutrals behave differently with indicatorsNever taste substances to test them
💡 Real-life connection: Invisible messages can be written using lemon juice; when an indicator or gentle heat is applied, the writing becomes visible due to a chemical change.

Litmus Test and Grouping Samples (Table 2.2)

A tiny strip of litmus paper can act like a detective for liquids!

Litmus paper is a common indicator used to test substances. Blue litmus turns red in acids, and red litmus turns blue in bases. If a sample does not change either litmus colour, it is likely neutral.

After testing several samples, we sort them into three groups: Group A (blue to red), Group B (red to blue), and Group C (no change). This careful grouping (like Table 2.2) helps us classify unknown samples using clear evidence.

Always record observations neatly, because exam questions often ask you to match results with groups and conclude whether a sample is acidic, basic, or neutral.

Acids turn blue litmus redBases turn red litmus blueNeutral samples do not change litmus
💡 Real-life connection: Testing soap water with red litmus: it often turns blue, showing the solution is basic.

✅ It turns red.

✅ It turns blue.
3. Which group contains samples that do not affect litmus paper?
✅ Group C.
4. A sample turns blue litmus red. Which group is it placed in?
✅ Group A.
5. Why is grouping results useful after testing many samples?
✅ It helps classify substances clearly as acidic, basic, or neutral.

Classifying Acidic, Basic, and Neutral Substances

Your kitchen holds acids and bases—without any lab coats needed!

After grouping with litmus, we name the nature of each group. Substances like lemon juice, amla juice, tamarind water, and vinegar turn blue litmus red, so they are acidic. Many cleaning solutions like soap water often turn red litmus blue, so they are basic.

Neutral substances do not change either litmus colour. Pure water is usually neutral, though impurities can change its nature. The key idea is: classification is based on indicator behaviour, not on taste or smell.

This classification matters in daily life: acids and bases are used in food, cleaning, agriculture, and medicines, so identifying them safely is important.

Lemon, amla, tamarind, vinegar are acidicSoap solutions are commonly basicNeutral means no litmus change
💡 Real-life connection: Sour curd and lemon juice taste sour because they contain acids, which also turn blue litmus red.

🧠 Try It Yourself

✅ Lemon juice and vinegar.
2. If a sample turns red litmus blue, what is it?
✅ Basic.
3. What does it mean if a sample shows no change with both litmus papers?
✅ It is neutral.
4. Why should we use indicators instead of taste?
✅ Tasting can be unsafe and harmful.
5. Write one use of acids or bases in daily life.
✅ Bases like soap help in cleaning.

Flower Extract as a Natural Indicator

Flowers can do chemistry—by changing colours like mood rings!

Some flower extracts act as natural indicators. When you add different samples to the extract, acidic solutions can shift the colour toward red shades, while basic solutions can shift it toward greenish shades (the exact colour depends on the flower used).

A useful check is to compare results with litmus grouping: samples that made blue litmus turn red (Group A) should also make the flower extract show an acidic colour change. Similarly, Group B should match the basic colour change, and Group C should show little or no change.

This comparison builds confidence in conclusions and teaches a key science skill: verifying results using more than one test.

Flower extracts can act as indicatorsAcids often shift colour toward red shadesCross-check results with litmus groups
💡 Real-life connection: A homemade indicator from coloured petals can be used to test lemon juice (acidic) and soap water (basic) and compare the colour changes.

🧠 Try It Yourself

1. What is a natural indicator mentioned in this section?
✅ Flower extract.
2. Which litmus group would likely turn flower extract reddish?
✅ Group A (acidic).
3. Which litmus group would likely turn flower extract greenish?
✅ Group B (basic).
4. Why compare flower extract results with litmus results?
✅ To verify classification using two indicators.
5. What should Group C do to the flower extract?
✅ Show no change or very little change.

      Fascinating Fact: Hydrangea and Soil Nature

      A plant can “read” the soil and change flower colour accordingly!

      Hydrangea plants can produce different flower colours depending on whether the soil is acidic or basic. In acidic soil, hydrangea flowers tend to become blue, while in basic soil they may become pink or red.

      This happens because soil chemistry affects how certain pigments and minerals behave inside the plant. It is a real-life example of how the nature of a substance (acidic/basic) can cause visible changes.

      Gardeners can sometimes influence the flower colour by changing soil conditions, but it must be done carefully to keep the plant healthy.

      Acidic soil can make hydrangea flowers blueBasic soil can make flowers pink or redSoil chemistry affects plant pigments
      💡 Real-life connection: A gardener tests soil and adjusts it to try to grow more blue hydrangea blooms by making the soil more acidic.

      🧠 Try It Yourself

      1. What flower colour is linked to acidic soil in hydrangea?
      ✅ Blue.
      2. What flower colour is linked to basic soil in hydrangea?
      ✅ Pink or red.
      3. What does this example show about acids and bases?
      ✅ They can cause visible colour changes in natural systems.
      4. What part of the environment affects hydrangea flower colour?
      ✅ The nature of the soil (acidic or basic).
      5. Why should soil changes be done carefully?
      ✅ To avoid harming the plant while adjusting soil nature.

      Turmeric Paper Indicator and the Curry-Stain Clue

      That yellow turmeric stain is actually a chemistry test in disguise!

      Turmeric can be used as an indicator by making turmeric paper. Turmeric usually stays yellow in acidic or neutral solutions, but it changes to a reddish-brown colour in basic solutions. So turmeric is especially useful for identifying bases.

      This explains a common observation: turmeric stains on clothes may change colour when soap is applied. Soap is basic, so it can turn the turmeric stain reddish-brown.

      When testing many samples, group the ones that do not change turmeric paper and compare them with Group A, B, and C from the litmus test. You will notice turmeric is best at spotting bases rather than acids.

      Turmeric turns reddish-brown in basesTurmeric usually remains yellow in acids and neutralsSoap changes turmeric stain due to basic nature
      💡 Real-life connection: A turmeric-stained shirt looks more reddish after rubbing soap, because the basic soap reacts with turmeric.

      🧠 Try It Yourself

      1. What colour change shows a base on turmeric paper?
      ✅ Yellow to reddish-brown.
      2. Does turmeric paper reliably identify acids? What happens in acids?
      ✅ No; it usually stays yellow in acids.
      3. Why does soap change the colour of a turmeric stain?
      ✅ Soap is basic and turns turmeric reddish-brown.
      4. If a sample changes turmeric paper to reddish-brown, it is likely what?
      ✅ Basic.
      5. What is the best way to confirm results: one indicator or more than one?
      ✅ More than one indicator.

      Holistic Lens: Lichens as Nature’s Air-Quality Clue

      Some living things act like environmental reporters—lichens are one of them!

      Lichens are formed by a close association between two organisms: a fungus and an alga. They often grow on rocks and tree bark, especially in places with abundant rainfall and clean air.

      Because lichens are sensitive to air pollution, their presence in your neighbourhood can hint at cleaner air. This is a “holistic lens” idea: science connects lab observations (like indicators) to real environments and living systems.

      Observation-based questions are common in exams: you may be asked what lichens indicate and where they are likely to be found.

      Lichens are formed by fungus and alga togetherThey grow well where air is cleanThey can indicate low pollution levels
      💡 Real-life connection: If many trees in a locality have lichens on their trunks, it may suggest the air there is relatively clean.

      🧠 Try It Yourself

      1. What two organisms form a lichen?
      ✅ A fungus and an alga.
      2. Where do lichens commonly grow?
      ✅ On rocks and tree bark.
      3. What does the presence of lichens often indicate?
      ✅ Clean air (low pollution).
      4. In which regions are lichens likely to be abundant, as mentioned?
      ✅ Regions with abundant rainfall and clean air.
      5. How is this section “holistic”?
      ✅ It links science learning to environmental observations in nature.

      Know a Scientist: Acharya P. C. Ray and Indian Chemistry

      One scientist’s dedication helped shape modern chemistry in India!

      Acharya Prafulla Chandra Ray (P. C. Ray) is called the “Father of Modern Indian Chemistry.” He earned a doctorate in chemistry in the United Kingdom and returned to India to strengthen scientific research.

      He also established India’s first pharmaceutical company in 1901, linking chemistry with public benefit through medicines. Along with research, he valued Indian culture and knowledge traditions and wrote about the history of chemistry in India.

      Learning about scientists reminds us that science grows through curiosity, careful work, and a desire to improve society.

      P. C. Ray is called the Father of Modern Indian ChemistryHe founded India’s first pharmaceutical company in 1901He supported research and wrote on chemistry history in India
      💡 Real-life connection: Medicines we use today depend on chemistry-based research and pharmaceutical development, fields supported early on by pioneers like P. C. Ray.
