📚 exploring substance acidic, basic and neutral — NCERT Q&A

Q1 In the science fair, words appeared on a white sheet when a liquid was sprayed on it. Which science concept introduced in this chapter can explain such colour changes, and why? ▼

The concept is the use of indicators to detect whether a substance is acidic, basic, or neutral. Step-by-step: 1) An indicator is a substance that shows a visible change (usually colour) when it comes in contact with an acid or a base. 2) In the fair activity, the “white sheet” likely had an indicator (or an indicator-like chemical) already present but invisible in normal conditions. 3) When the liquid was sprayed, it acted as an acid or a base (or contained one). 4) The indicator reacted with the sprayed liquid and changed colour in those areas, making the hidden writing visible. 5) This is similar to litmus changing colour in acids or bases, which is what the chapter begins to explore.

Q2 Activity 2.1 uses both blue litmus paper and red litmus paper. Why is it necessary to test a sample with both colours of litmus? ▼

Testing with both blue and red litmus helps you correctly identify whether the sample is acidic, basic, or neutral. Step-by-step: 1) Blue litmus turns red only in acidic solutions. 2) Red litmus turns blue only in basic solutions. 3) If you use only blue litmus and there is no change, the sample could be either basic or neutral (both keep blue litmus unchanged). 4) If you use only red litmus and there is no change, the sample could be either acidic or neutral (both keep red litmus unchanged). 5) Using both resolves the confusion: - Acid: blue → red; red stays red. - Base: red → blue; blue stays blue. - Neutral: no change in either.

Q3 How will you group the samples after testing with litmus papers as described in the chapter (Group A, B, and C)? Also state what each group represents. ▼

Grouping is based on how each sample affects blue and red litmus. Step-by-step: 1) Group A: Put in Group A those samples that turn blue litmus paper to red. - This indicates the sample is acidic. 2) Group B: Put in Group B those samples that turn red litmus paper to blue. - This indicates the sample is basic. 3) Group C: Put in Group C those samples that do not change the colour of either blue or red litmus. - This indicates the sample is neutral (neither acidic nor basic).

Using common properties and the litmus rule: Step-by-step: 1) Acidic (turn blue litmus red): - Lemon juice (sour) - Amla juice (sour) - Tamarind water (sour) - Vinegar (sour) 2) Basic (turn red litmus blue): - Soap solution (slippery/basic) - Baking soda solution (mild base) - Lime water (calcium hydroxide solution, basic) - Washing powder solution (generally basic) 3) Neutral (no change to either litmus): - Tap water (usually near neutral in daily life) - Sugar solution - Salt solution Note: Exact results can depend on concentration and impurities, but this is the expected classification for school-level testing.

Q5 Define litmus. From where is it obtained, and why is it called an acid-base indicator? ▼

Litmus is a natural dye-like substance obtained from lichens. Step-by-step: 1) Source: Litmus is extracted from lichens (organisms formed by association of a fungus and an alga). 2) Forms: It is available as a solution and also on paper strips called litmus paper. 3) Colours: Litmus paper is commonly available in two colours—blue litmus and red litmus. 4) Indicator role: It shows different colours in acidic and basic solutions: - In acid: blue litmus turns red. - In base: red litmus turns blue. 5) Because it helps identify acids and bases by colour change, it is called an acid-base indicator.

Q6 Write the correct observations for an acidic substance and for a basic substance when tested with blue and red litmus papers. ▼

Observations with litmus are as follows: Step-by-step: 1) For an acidic substance: - Blue litmus: turns red. - Red litmus: remains red (no change). 2) For a basic substance: - Red litmus: turns blue. - Blue litmus: remains blue (no change).

Q7 The chapter warns: ‘Do not confuse lime water with lime (the fruit).’ What is lime water, and how is it prepared as per the text? ▼

Lime water is a solution of calcium hydroxide in water, and it is basic in nature. Step-by-step preparation (as per the chapter): 1) Take ‘lime’ (chuna), which is calcium oxide. 2) Mix it in water. 3) Leave the mixture undisturbed for some time (about an hour). 4) After the solid settles, filter the clear liquid into another container. 5) The filtered clear liquid is lime water (calcium hydroxide solution).

Q8 What are lichens, and what does their presence in an area generally indicate (as given in the chapter’s holistic lens)? ▼

Lichens are formed by the association of two living organisms—a fungus and an alga. Step-by-step: 1) Nature of lichens: They live together in a mutually beneficial association. 2) Where they grow: They commonly grow on rocks and trees. 3) Environmental condition: They grow well in regions with abundant rainfall and clean air. 4) Therefore, their presence generally indicates cleaner air (low pollution) in that area.

Q2 Define the term ‘indicator’. Explain why litmus is called an acid-base indicator. ▼

Indicator: An indicator is a substance that shows a visible change (usually colour) when added to an acidic or basic solution. Why litmus is an acid-base indicator (step-by-step): 1) Litmus exists in two colours: blue and red. 2) In acidic solutions, blue litmus changes to red. 3) In basic solutions, red litmus changes to blue. 4) Because litmus gives different colour responses in acids and bases, it helps identify whether a substance is acidic or basic. 5) Hence, litmus is called an acid-base indicator.

Q4 A student tests four solutions with litmus and records: (i) Blue litmus turns red; red litmus shows no change. (ii) Red litmus turns blue; blue litmus shows no change. (iii) Neither red nor blue litmus changes colour. (iv) Blue litmus turns red and red litmus turns blue. For (i)–(iii), name the nature of the solution. For (iv), state whether the observation is possible and give a reason. ▼

Step-by-step: (i) Blue → red; red unchanged: - Nature: Acidic. Reason: Acids turn blue litmus red and do not turn red litmus blue. (ii) Red → blue; blue unchanged: - Nature: Basic. Reason: Bases turn red litmus blue and do not turn blue litmus red. (iii) No change in either: - Nature: Neutral. Reason: Neutral solutions do not affect either litmus. (iv) Blue → red and red → blue: - This observation is not possible for a single sample under normal conditions. Reason: A solution cannot be strongly acidic and strongly basic at the same time. If both changes are seen, it suggests an error such as mixing up samples, using contaminated droppers, or applying different liquids on different strips.

Q5 Explain Activity 2.1 in your own words. Write the precautions to be followed while performing the litmus test on different samples. ▼

Activity 2.1 (procedure in steps): 1) Collect small samples: lemon juice, soap solution, amla juice, tamarind water, vinegar, baking soda solution, lime water, tap water, washing powder solution, sugar solution, salt solution (and any other). 2) Cut blue litmus paper into small pieces. 3) Place the pieces on a clean, dry white tile. 4) Using a dropper, put one drop of each sample on separate blue litmus pieces. 5) Observe and note colour changes. 6) Repeat the same steps using red litmus paper pieces. 7) Record observations and then group samples into: - Group A: blue → red (acidic) - Group B: red → blue (basic) - Group C: no change (neutral) Precautions (exam-ready): 1) Use a clean dropper for each sample or wash the dropper thoroughly before using it for the next sample to avoid contamination. 2) Keep the tile clean and dry; moisture can mix drops and affect results. 3) Put only one drop on each litmus piece; excess liquid may spread and mix. 4) Label litmus pieces or place them at a distance so samples do not mix. 5) Do not touch litmus with wet hands; water can affect the paper. 6) Use fresh litmus paper; old or damp strips may give unclear colour changes. 7) Note observations immediately, as colour may fade after some time.

Q8 A student says: ‘If a solution does not change blue litmus paper, it must be neutral.’ Do you agree? Explain with reasons and an example from the given list of samples. ▼

No, the statement is not correct. Step-by-step reasoning: 1) Blue litmus changes to red only in acidic solutions. 2) If blue litmus shows no change, the solution could be: - Basic (because bases keep blue litmus blue), OR - Neutral (neutral solutions also do not change blue litmus). 3) Therefore, ‘no change in blue litmus’ is not enough to call it neutral. Example: - Soap solution is basic, yet it does not change blue litmus (it remains blue). It changes red litmus to blue. Correct conclusion: - To confirm neutral, the sample must show no change with both red and blue litmus papers.

Q12 A dropper used for lemon juice was accidentally used for soap solution without washing. What error can this cause in litmus testing? Explain how you would avoid this error in the activity. ▼

Possible error due to contamination: Step-by-step: 1) Lemon juice is acidic, while soap solution is basic. 2) If the dropper carries lemon juice into soap solution, the soap sample may become partially neutralised. 3) Then the litmus change may become weak, delayed, or incorrect (for example, red litmus may not turn clearly blue, or blue litmus might show unexpected behaviour). 4) This leads to wrong grouping (acidic/basic/neutral) and wrong conclusions. How to avoid: 1) Use separate droppers for each sample, OR 2) Wash the dropper properly with water and dry it before taking the next sample. 3) Also keep sample containers covered and labelled to prevent mixing.
